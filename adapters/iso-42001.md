# ISO/IEC 42001:2023

Mapping of SEAT outcomes to ISO/IEC 42001 AI management system clauses, centered on the competence, awareness, leadership, and performance-evaluation requirements that a security awareness program provides audit evidence for.

**Version:** 2023  
**Adapter last updated:** 2026-07-22  
**Official reference:** https://www.iso.org/standard/42001  
**Adapter id:** `iso-42001`

## How an assistant applies this adapter

You need the respondent's per-question scores (1-5) from `assessment/questions.md`, produced by the flow in `AGENTS.md`. Then:

1. **Compute each outcome score.** Every question carries a SEAT outcome id (see `adapters/outcomes.md`). For an outcome, average the scores of its questions, skipping any answered N/A, and round to one decimal. An outcome with no answered questions scores 0.
2. **Decide met or gap, per assurance level.** The threshold depends on the mapping's assurance level, not a single global number:
   - Required: outcome score >= 4.5
   - Expected: outcome score >= 2.5
   - Recommended: outcome score >= 1.5
   An outcome is met when its score is at or above the threshold for that row, otherwise it is a gap.
3. **List missing evidence.** For any unmet row whose assurance level is Required or Expected, report that row's evidence types as missing evidence, together with its framework reference.
4. **Compute the overall alignment score.** For each row the effective score is the outcome score when met. When not met it is `(outcome score / 5) * threshold * 0.5`, the penalty the platform applies. Multiply each effective score by the row weight (default 1), sum, divide by total weight, round to one decimal. The result is on a 0 to 5 scale, not a percentage.

Report met, gap, and missing evidence per requirement, then the overall score. Do not invent requirements that are not in the table below, and never answer the assessment questions on the respondent's behalf.

## Mapping notes

**Strategic Alignment (Clause 4.1, 4.2, 5.2).** Clause 5.2 requires an AI policy aligned to the organization context (4.1) and interested-party needs (4.2). A program strategy that ties workforce AI literacy to the organization's actual AI use cases is what makes that policy operational rather than a framed statement on the wall.

**Governance Documentation (Clause 5.2, Annex A.2, Annex A.3).** Annex A.2 (policies related to AI) and A.3 (internal organization, roles and responsibilities) require documented ownership. If nobody can produce the page naming who owns AI acceptable use and workforce training, the AIMS fails the documentation test an auditor applies first.

**Executive Support (Clause 5.1).** Clause 5.1 makes top management demonstrate leadership and commitment to the AIMS, including resourcing it. Evidence that leadership reviewed AI risk and funded the program is a direct certification artifact, not a nice-to-have.

**Continuous Improvement (Clause 9.3, 10.1).** Clause 10.1 requires continual improvement of the AIMS and 9.3 requires management review to drive it. A program that revises its AI guidance on a cadence and after incidents produces the improvement record surveillance audits look for.

**Targeted Communication (Clause 7.3, 7.4).** Clause 7.4 requires determining internal communications relevant to the AIMS and 7.3 requires awareness of the AI policy and each person's contribution. Role-targeted communication is how both are met for a workforce rather than a single all-staff memo.

**Cultural Relevance (Clause 7.3).** Clause 7.3 awareness is only real when people understand why the AI policy matters to their work. Messaging that resonates across roles is what turns required awareness into a culture that questions AI output.

**Active Participation (Clause 10.2, Annex A.9).** Annex A.9 (responsible use of AI systems) and clause 10.2 (nonconformity and corrective action) depend on people surfacing problems. A workforce that reports misuse or unexpected AI behavior feeds the nonconformity process the standard requires.

**Feedback Responsiveness (Clause 10.2).** Clause 10.2 requires reacting to nonconformities and correcting them. If staff report AI problems and nothing changes, the corrective-action loop is not operating and the finding writes itself.

**Performance Measurement (Clause 9.1).** Clause 9.1 requires the organization to determine what to monitor and measure about the AIMS and to evaluate its performance and effectiveness. SEAT maturity measurement over time is the effectiveness evidence for the workforce controls, which is what a certification auditor asks to see.

**Risk Driven Assessment (Clause 6.1, Annex A.5).** Clause 6.1 requires an AI risk assessment and treatment process, and Annex A.5 requires AI system impact assessments. Program effort should follow that risk picture, concentrating on the AI uses with the highest impact rather than treating every use the same.

**Relevant Tailored Training (Clause 7.2).** Clause 7.2 requires that people doing work affecting AIMS performance are competent and that the organization retains evidence of competence. Role-appropriate training is the mechanism, and training records are the retained evidence.

**Effective Learning Methods (Clause 7.2).** Clause 7.2 asks the organization to take actions to acquire competence and to evaluate the effectiveness of those actions. Methods that demonstrably change behavior, not just record attendance, are what satisfy the effectiveness half of that clause.

**Accessible Inclusive Training (Clause 7.3).** Clause 7.3 awareness applies to persons doing work under the organization's control, which includes contractors and staff outside the AI team. Training that reaches everyone in scope is what keeps the awareness control from having gaps an auditor can sample into.

**Integrated Training Lifecycle (Clause 7.2, Annex A.4).** Annex A.4 (resources for AI systems, including competence) and clause 7.2 expect competence to be maintained across the lifecycle. Embedding AI competence from onboarding and refreshing it on role change keeps the control current rather than a one-time event.
