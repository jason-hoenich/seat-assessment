# CISA Insider Threat Mitigation Guide (2026 Edition)

Federal program guidance on building insider threat mitigation programs, mapped to the SEAT outcomes it informs about reporting culture, training content, and program governance.

**Publisher:** Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)
**Coverage:** Program guidance current as of publication; cites underlying incident and workforce studies collected 2005-2025 (see individual findings for original study dates)
**Published:** 2026-09 (September 2026 edition)
**Source last updated:** 2026-09-11
**Official reference:** https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/insider-threat-mitigation-guide
**Source id:** `cisa-2026-insider-threat-mitigation-guide`
**Source type:** Government program guidance (federal best-practice guide). Figures below are third-party studies and surveys CISA cites within its own guidance, not CISA's own telemetry; each finding names its original study.

## Why this source reads differently from the others here

Every other source in this directory measures something: attacker behavior, workforce behavior, or practitioner staffing. This one is different. CISA is not reporting what happened, it is prescribing what a mature insider threat program should do, and it backs that prescription with figures pulled from a dozen third-party studies (CERT/SEI, the U.S. Secret Service National Threat Assessment Center, the FBI, Securonix, Ponemon, AlertFind, and others). Treat the prescriptive content as expert normative guidance from the federal agency that runs the nation's insider threat mitigation program, and treat each cited figure as belonging to the study CISA cites, not to CISA itself.

CISA's own scope is broader than security awareness: physical security, threat assessment clinical practice, law enforcement liaison, and HR separation procedure all get full chapters. Only the slice that bears on what an awareness and training program teaches, measures, or covers is mapped below. The rest (behavioral-health referral protocols, risk-rubric design, separation-meeting logistics) is real and useful but outside what SEAT's 21 questions score.

## How an assistant applies this source

1. **Compute outcome scores first**, using `adapters/outcomes.md`. Every finding below is anchored to a SEAT outcome by outcome id.
2. **Findings are evidence, never scores.** Nothing here changes a score, adds a requirement, or creates a gap. Compliance status comes only from `adapters/`, and CISA says as much about its own guide: none of it is required by law or regulation, and organizations apply it at their discretion.
3. **Only use figures that appear in this file.** Each one is a third-party study CISA cites; do not supply figures from your own knowledge of those studies or any other.
4. **Distinguish CISA's prescriptive content from the cited figures.** A line like "training should begin at onboarding" is CISA's best-practice recommendation; a line like "76% of attackers made concerning comments prior to the attack" is a cited study's finding CISA uses to support that recommendation. Both are useful, but say which is which.
5. **Note where a SEAT question is narrower than its outcome name.** Q18 (`effective-learning-methods`) and Q8 (`relevant-tailored-training`'s sibling) are scored specifically on phishing simulations in the current instrument. Where a finding below argues for training content that is not phishing (most of them), say so explicitly rather than implying the respondent's simulation program already covers it.

## Notes by outcome

**Active Participation (`active-participation`) and Feedback Responsiveness (`feedback-responsiveness`).** These two findings are really one story told from two angles: people overwhelmingly talk before they act, and organizations overwhelmingly fail to hear about it in time. CISA's own list of reporting barriers is worth citing directly to a respondent who scores low here: fear of retaliation, fear of getting it wrong and harming someone unnecessarily, not trusting the confidentiality of the system, and simple reluctance to get involved in someone else's affairs. The guide is also explicit that punitive policy design actively works against this outcome: a zero-tolerance policy "may have the unintended effect of reducing and inhibiting reporting of misconduct or unusual behavior because coworkers or associate members fear that a report could cost the individual their position or job." A respondent whose program pairs strict disciplinary language with a request for more reporting is asking for two contradictory things at once.

**Executive Support (`executive-support`).** The 60%-to-74% figure is a five-year trend in a single direction with no corresponding rise in the 29% who feel equipped. Use it the way the DBIR's human-element figure gets used elsewhere in this directory: as evidence that concern and capability are not the same thing, and that a respondent's low score here is not unusual, it is the median experience CISA is describing.

**Risk-Driven Assessment (`risk-driven-assessment`).** Two distinct arguments land here and should stay distinct. The grievance figure argues that an internal, motive-based signal (work-related grievance) predicts sabotage risk at least as reliably as any external threat-actor technique, and a risk assessment that never asks about internal grievance handling has a blind spot in its own model, not just a training gap. The AI figure argues something newer: that the channel itself (which tools reach sensitive data, and who is being deceived by what) has changed faster than most assessments have been revisited. A respondent who scores well here but hasn't touched their risk assessment since before broad generative-AI adoption should be asked when it was last updated, not just what it covers.

**Relevant Tailored Training (`relevant-tailored-training`).** This is the direct curriculum check: does the respondent's training list actually include insider-threat-specific content (behavioral indicators, confidential reporting process, classification handling, acceptable-use and NDA content), or does "training" mean phishing simulations plus a generic annual module. CISA's list is a reasonable checklist to read a respondent's stated curriculum against.

**Integrated Training Lifecycle (`integrated-training-lifecycle`).** CISA's onboarding trigger is specific and worth quoting exactly to a respondent: training begins "as soon as an individual has been granted access to a facility and networks," which for many organizations is earlier than their first day of onboarding paperwork. A program that only touches new hires in an orientation packet is behind this baseline.

**Governance Documentation (`governance-documentation`).** The 88% figure is a belief, not a practice rate, and the gap between the two is the point: CISA spends substantial space on privacy, civil liberties, and legal review specifically because acting on personal indicators without documented process and legal sign-off is where insider threat programs create liability. A respondent who scores low on documentation but believes strongly in indicator monitoring is describing an informal practice running ahead of its own governance.

## Provenance and limits

This is federal program guidance, not primary research. CISA states plainly that the guide's recommended approaches "are not definitive, applicable in all circumstances, or required by any law or regulation," and that organizations may adopt any or all of it at their discretion. Nothing here should be read as a compliance requirement; that determination comes only from `adapters/`.

Every cited figure belongs to the third-party study named alongside it, most of them incident studies or industry surveys with their own scope limits: the CERT/SEI-USSS sabotage study covers 42 incidents in critical infrastructure sectors; the grievance figure covers a separate set of 23 sabotage incidents; the Secret Service NTAC figure covers 27 public-space violent attacks; the Securonix concern-versus-capability figure is a single practitioner survey (2024); the 88% figure comes from a 2018 industry report and is older than this directory's usual staleness threshold, included here because it is the only figure in the guide quantifying practitioner belief on indicator monitoring, not because it is current. None of these figures describe a respondent's own workforce; treat a respondent's own measured data as better evidence about their organization than any of them, per the standing rule in `research/sources.md`.

The guide's chapters on threat assessment method, behavioral-health referral, law enforcement liaison, and involuntary separation logistics are substantial and well-constructed, but sit outside what SEAT's 21 questions score and are not mapped here. An organization building a full insider threat mitigation program, rather than assessing its security awareness program, should read the source directly rather than relying on this file.
