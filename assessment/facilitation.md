# Facilitation Guide

Per-question context for whoever is administering this assessment, human or LLM. Each entry has three parts: why the question exists, what strong actually looks like, and the trap that makes people over-score themselves. Share the context before asking the question. Offer the tip when someone is stuck between two levels or answering too fast.

The standing rule from AGENTS.md applies: when in doubt between two levels, record the lower one. The gap is where the value is.

---

## Strategy

### Q1: Strategic Alignment

**Why this matters:** Programs that can't tie their work to business risk are the ones that lose budget in year two. This question separates "we do training because compliance says so" from "we reduce risks the business actually carries."

**What good looks like:** At level 4 or 5, you can name the organizational risks your program addresses, and someone outside security has agreed those are the right ones. There's a documented line from awareness activity to a risk register entry.

**Honest-scoring tip:** A mention of the awareness program in a risk document you didn't write doesn't count as alignment. If nobody reviews the connection, you're at 2, maybe 3.

### Q2: Governance and Documentation

**Why this matters:** Undocumented programs die with the person running them. Documentation is also the first thing an auditor, a regulator, or a new CISO asks for, and the answer sets the tone for everything after.

**What good looks like:** Roles, responsibilities, cadence, and scope written down, findable, and current. Someone new could pick up the program from the docs alone.

**Honest-scoring tip:** A slide deck from two years ago is history, and history isn't documentation. If the docs haven't been touched since the last leadership change, score lower.

### Q3: Executive Support

**Why this matters:** SANS 2026 puts a program that embeds culture at 4.3 or more full-time people, and at least 3 before behavior moves at all. Most orgs run it as a fraction of one person's job. The distance between those two numbers is executive support, or the lack of it.

**What good looks like:** Leaders show up in the program (recorded intros, town hall mentions, visible participation), and the budget survives planning season without a fight.

**Honest-scoring tip:** "Our CISO approves the budget" is level 2 or 3 behavior. Advocacy means leadership spends its own credibility on the program, unprompted, more than once a year.

### Q4: Continuous Assessment

**Why this matters:** A program that never examines itself can only repeat itself. Regular assessment is how you find out whether last year's plan did anything before you commit to running it again.

**What good looks like:** A recurring, structured review that includes the awareness program's contribution to security posture, with findings that change the next cycle's plan.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Counting this assessment as your assessment practice is circular. If the last comprehensive look before today was more than a year ago, that's a 2.

### Q6: Compliance vs. Risk Orientation

**Why this matters:** Compliance sets the floor, and programs built only to the floor stay there. This question asks what actually drives your content calendar: the audit checklist or the threats hitting your organization.

**What good looks like:** Compliance requirements are embedded in a program shaped by your threat landscape, so the audit passes as a side effect rather than as the goal.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Look at your last three training topics. If each one traces to a regulation rather than to a risk decision, answer as compliance-driven no matter what the program charter says.

### Q7: Threat-Informed Content

**Why this matters:** Employees notice when training describes attacks nobody has ever seen at your company. Content built from your own phishing reports, SOC tickets, and incidents earns attention that generic content never will.

**What good looks like:** A repeatable pipeline from threat intel and incident data into training and simulations, so this quarter's content reflects this quarter's attacks.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Mentioning a famous breach in the annual training is level 2. The question is whether your own organization's incident data changes what you teach, and how quickly.

## Engagement

### Q9: Audience Targeting

**Why this matters:** Finance sees invoice fraud, engineering sees credential attacks, executives see whaling. One-size-fits-all training fits nobody, and the highest-risk groups are exactly the ones generic content misses.

**What good looks like:** Defined personas or role groups, each with content matched to the attacks they actually face, and a way to know the right content reached the right group.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Sending the same course to everyone with a different due date is scheduling, and scheduling isn't targeting. If role changes nothing about what a person sees, score 1 or 2.

### Q10: Recognition and Incentives

**Why this matters:** Programs that only ever punish clicking teach people to hide mistakes. Recognition flips the incentive: reporting a phish becomes something people want to be seen doing.

**What good looks like:** Visible, meaningful recognition for the behaviors you want repeated, first reporters, champions, teams with strong reporting instinct, sustained over time rather than a one-week campaign.

**Honest-scoring tip:** A leaderboard nobody looks at doesn't move behavior. Ask when recognition was last given and whether the recipient's manager heard about it. If you can't recall, score lower.

### Q11: Communication Reach

**Why this matters:** The people hardest to reach (frontline, deskless, shift workers, field teams) are often the most exposed. A program that only lands in corporate email covers the part of the org that was already easiest to protect.

**What good looks like:** Multiple channels matched to how each population actually works, with some check that messages arrive and register at every level, top floor to shop floor.

**Honest-scoring tip:** "We sent it to everyone" measures sending. If you've never verified what non-desk employees received or understood, you don't know your reach, and the score should say so.

### Q12: Cultural and Language Fit

**Why this matters:** Training that ignores language and culture reads as an import from headquarters. People disengage from content that clearly wasn't made for them, and disengaged people don't change behavior.

**What good looks like:** Content available in the languages your workforce actually speaks, with examples and tone that fit how your organization talks, not just translated word-for-word.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Auto-translated captions on an English video is a start, and only a start. If a non-English-speaking employee would describe the program as "not for me," score 2.

### Q13: Feedback Loops

**Why this matters:** Without a feedback channel, you learn about disengagement from your metrics a quarter late. Employees are the first to know when content is stale, patronizing, or missing the attacks they see. Ask them.

**What good looks like:** An easy way to give feedback, evidence people use it, and at least one visible change the program made because of it.

**Honest-scoring tip:** A survey nobody reads collects data without collecting insight. The test is whether you can name a change driven by employee feedback in the last year. No example, lower score.

## Assessment

### Q5: Metrics and Impact

**Why this matters:** 84% of organizations measure effectiveness by training completion, which measures activity, and activity was never the goal. This question asks whether you can show the program changes behavior and reduces risk.

**What good looks like:** Metrics tied to outcomes: reporting rates, repeat-click trends, behavior change over time, connected to risk reduction a leadership audience would accept as evidence.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Completion rate, open rate, and attendance are delivery metrics. If removing them leaves your dashboard empty, you're at 2, whatever the dashboard looks like.

### Q8: Simulation Realism

**Why this matters:** Employees learn to spot your simulations instead of real attacks when the simulations look nothing like what's hitting your inboxes. Realism decides whether the exercise builds a reflex or a rumor.

**What good looks like:** Simulations modeled on attacks your organization actually receives, varied in difficulty, updated as the real threat mix shifts.

**Honest-scoring tip:** If your last three simulations came straight from a vendor's default template library, untouched, answer as generic. Bonus check: do employees joke that they can always tell which ones are yours?

### Q14: Self-Assessment Practice

**Why this matters:** Maturity is a trajectory, and you only see a trajectory when you measure the same way more than once. Regular self-assessment turns "we think we improved" into a baseline you can defend.

**What good looks like:** A recurring maturity review on a schedule, same instrument each time, with results that feed the roadmap and get shared beyond the security team.

**Honest-scoring tip:** If this is your first structured look at the program, welcome, and score it as your first: a 1 or 2 on this question, which is exactly the point of a baseline.

### Q15: Risk-Driven Improvement

**Why this matters:** Threats move. A program that sets its plan in January and ignores what happens after is defending last year's org chart against last year's attacks.

**What good looks like:** Risk and vulnerability findings flow into the program on a cycle: new risk identified, program adjusts, adjustment verified.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Name the last program change a risk assessment caused. If the answer is a shrug, the assessments and the program are running in parallel, and parallel scores a 2.

### Q16: Reporting and Dashboards

**Why this matters:** Data that lives in a spreadsheet only you can open doesn't influence anyone. Accessible reporting is how the program's evidence reaches the people who fund it and the leaders who act on it.

**What good looks like:** KPIs visible to stakeholders on demand, current enough to trust, clear enough that a non-security exec draws the right conclusion without a walkthrough.

**Honest-scoring tip:** A quarterly email attachment is level 2 or 3 reporting. Ask whether anyone outside your team has looked at the numbers without you presenting them.

## Training

### Q17: Risk-Aligned Materials

**Why this matters:** Training time is the scarcest resource the program spends, and it spends someone else's. Content aligned to identified risks buys risk reduction; content chosen from a catalog buys completion records.

**What good looks like:** Each training topic traces to an identified risk or audience need, and high-risk groups get depth where the risk actually sits.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Open your training plan and ask "which risk put this module here?" for each line. Every module without an answer pulls the score down.

### Q18: Simulations as Education

**Why this matters:** A phishing test that ends at "you clicked" is a trap, and people treat traps with resentment. The click is the teachable moment; what happens in the next sixty seconds decides whether anyone learns.

**What good looks like:** Immediate, blame-free education at the moment of failure, difficulty that builds skills progressively, and reporting celebrated as the win condition.

**Honest-scoring tip:** If clicking triggers mandatory remedial training framed as a consequence, that's punishment wearing an education costume. Employees know the difference, and so should the score.

### Q19: Accessibility and Inclusion

**Why this matters:** Every employee who can't access the training in their language, with their assistive tech, on their device, is an employee the program has silently decided not to protect.

**What good looks like:** Materials that work across languages, abilities, and devices your workforce actually uses, verified rather than assumed.

**Honest-scoring tip:** "Nobody has complained" is absence of feedback, and absence of feedback usually means the excluded people gave up quietly. Check coverage against your actual workforce before scoring above 3.

### Q20: Delivery Cadence

**Why this matters:** One annual training loses to the forgetting curve every time. Behavior change comes from spaced repetition: smaller, regular touches that keep the instinct warm between big moments.

**What good looks like:** A published rhythm people can anticipate: recurring short-form content through the year, with room to break cadence when a live threat needs a fast message.

**Honest-scoring tip:** Annual compliance training plus ad-hoc panic emails is a 2. A cadence you couldn't sketch on a whiteboard from memory isn't a cadence yet.

### Q21: Lifecycle Integration

**Why this matters:** Day one is the highest-attention moment an employee ever gives you, and role changes are when their risk profile shifts. A program bolted onto the calendar misses both.

**What good looks like:** Security woven into onboarding from day one, refreshed at role changes and promotions, and present in development plans so security fluency grows with seniority.

**Honest-scoring tip:** A security slide inside HR's onboarding deck is presence without integration. If nothing security-related happens between onboarding and the next annual training, score 2.
